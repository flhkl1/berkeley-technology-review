Section 1

Executive summary

Generalist AI, Inc. aims to solve the problem of the time and cost friction associated with programming robots for specific tasks.

Their solution is an embodied foundation model that allows robots to rapidly learn new behaviours and accomplish a wide set of tasks — even tasks that were not explicitly programmed into the robot a priori. GEN-1.5 citation TK

Section 2

Technology

An embodied foundation model refers to a model, trained on a broad spectrum of data, to be generally intelligent (“foundation”) in the context of perceiving, reasoning about and acting on our physical world (“embodied”).

There are five words worth giving intuition to, in order.

A model is a machine that takes something in and spits something out. It takes as input a state of the world — for example a bundle of data that includes video captured from a camera, audio captured from a microphone, and sensor readings captured from a gripper, all timestamped so they correspond to each other — and outputs a set of actions that a robot might take. One can think of this machine as having many knobs, or weights, that affect how the machine reacts to certain inputs.

We are interested in this machine reacting in ways that are productive. Consider three machines given the task of slicing a cucumber.

The natural idea is to see whether we can train our machine to act like Machine A.

The key insight is that this machine has many knobs, or weights, that can be tuned — trained — by some set of instructions, a recipe, so that our final machine will act more like Machine A. But do we know which knobs to tune? These models have billions of parameters, or knobs. It seems really hard, and expensive.

Luckily, we have a class of algorithms called optimizers. You may have heard of gradient descent. One can think of an optimizer as a recipe that takes a score we calculated — a score measuring how unhappy we are with the model’s performance on the tasks we chose to evaluate it on — and returns instructions, by way of the chain rule, for how to tune every weight in the model to improve. Follow the recipe many times, and the model generally does better on the measure of performance we set. Each pass is called an iteration.

Unluckily, this is still expensive.1

The key differentiator between everything previously mentioned and Generalist — and the other physical-AI foundation model companies — is that they are concerned with building models that interact with the real world. Contrast this with Claude and GPT, which deal, in the words of Peter Thiel, with the world of bits as opposed to the world of atoms.2 It means their AI must act as the brain of something that exists in the physical world, embodied. For the technically inclined

The same section, stated in the vocabulary of the field

Machine learning follows a predictable pattern.

A model is a universal function approximator. If a function is any set of input–output relationships, then the problem of intelligently navigating through reality can be viewed as a massively multidimensional distribution to be learned. Historically this is done by defining a notion of loss — cross-entropy, for instance — and executing an optimization algorithm such as Adam3 that iteratively adjusts parameters on what can be thought of as a loss landscape, so as to minimize that loss. Figure 3 is that landscape.

There are many model architectures, and the choice of one carries an inductive bias: the set of assumptions the architecture makes about the problem, which should make it easier to solve.

Architecture Assumption it builds in What that buys
Convolutional network (CNN) Spatial locality — pixels close to each other are closely related. Strong performance on images, where the assumption happens to be true.
Transformer Through attention: every token can be related to every other token, with no prior statement of which relationships matter. Which relationships matter is learned rather than assumed — a low inductive bias, and so a wide range of tasks.

The transformer4 turns out to be great for two reasons: hardware compatibility with the units of compute, GPUs, that were most available at the outset of the generative-AI boom; and low inductive bias.

The first rests on an empirical observation — that scaling laws can be exploited. Scaling laws are an architecture-agnostic phenomenon: as you scale up compute and data, performance improves predictably.5 Later work found that compute-optimal training scales model size and training tokens in roughly equal proportion — a doubling of parameters should correspond to a doubling of training tokens.6 All of this while exploiting the parallel processing capabilities of the GPUs NVIDIA was shipping at the time.

The second means transformer architectures can be adapted, with minimal effort, to many different tasks — including the task of deciding what set of actions to take given data perceived from the world, such as the video and audio a robot receives.

Editorial standards

We do the best work in this domain of anybody.

We mix boots-on-the-ground investigative journalism — primary sources — with deep technical knowledge and data-driven analysis, from cleaned, verified, publicly available pipelines, distilled and presented beautifully. We want to attract the best minds in journalism, finance and technology to help support this mission. It is all pro bono.

Origination
What we cover is arbitrary, and is decided by authors who follow the frontier.
Accuracy
Everything in a report is factually accurate and cited.
Frontier
We commit to covering, in some capacity, developments that happen on the frontier — which we define, arbitrarily, as within the last week.
Authorship
Research with AI is fine. We commit to writing and reviewing every word by hand.
Audience
Our readers are individuals seeking to know more about what we cover. The writing should not contain overt jargon, and the thesis should be legible to the general public. We accept the friction of accurately distilling complex technologies for non-technical audiences.
Presentation
Every report produces an entry on this site, with relevant and visually appealing graphics and a cited whitepaper. The presentation should itself be on the frontier.
